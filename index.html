Personal statement

What building taught me.

I did not come into software through the conventional route. I taught myself by building things that were difficult enough to expose what I did not know.

The trading system tested that more than anything else. There were stretches where it felt relentless. Fix one problem, uncover another. Get something working, then discover the real problem was somewhere else entirely. Hours could disappear into something that looked as though it should have taken minutes.

That was enormously frustrating. At first, frustration made me want to move faster — change more, try another fix, force the problem to give way. Eventually I learned that good engineering often demands the opposite.

Stop. Keep calm. Protect what already works. Understand the failure. Change one thing. Test it properly. Then move again.

That change in me mattered more than any particular framework or programming language. I became more patient, more methodical and less frightened by something I did not understand yet. I learned that being stuck is not the same as being beaten.

Software can be stubborn. That does not mean I cannot build software. I can be more stubborn.

Not stubborn enough to keep making the same mistake. Stubborn enough to keep learning until I understand it.

How I learned to build software

Self-taught. AI-assisted. Evidence-led.

I am self-taught.

I did not come into software with a computer science degree, a graduate scheme or years inside an engineering department. I started with problems I wanted to solve and learned what I needed in order to solve them.

I also learned at a time when something fundamentally different had become possible: I could work alongside AI.

ChatGPT became part teacher, part technical partner, part sounding board and, occasionally, the thing I argued with until we found the real problem.

AI helped me move faster, but AI did not remove the need to understand what I was building. If anything, it made judgement more important. I still had to decide what the system should do, recognise when an answer was wrong, protect working code, test changes, investigate failures and decide when something was genuinely ready.

That is how these projects were built.

Not by one person knowing everything, but by one person learning how to work with AI well enough to tackle things he could not have tackled alone a few years earlier.

I do not claim that means I can walk into any project already knowing every answer.

I believe something more useful:

Give me a problem, the right AI tools and enough time to understand it, and I am very difficult to stop.

I may not have the conventional letters after my name.

Mine are GPT.

And I have the working software, failed experiments, recovered systems, tests, audits and engineering decisions to show what that collaboration can produce.

Evidence standard

A claim only gets stronger when the evidence does.

The Hub distinguishes shipped/live evidence, current repository evidence, historical documentation and work still requiring re-verification.

Case-file format

  • Problem: what failed, mattered or needed a deliberate trade-off.
  • Constraint: the technical, safety, cost or delivery condition.
  • Decision: the architecture or implementation choice.
  • Evidence: repository reference, test, release, audit or live demonstration.
  • Limitation: what remains unproven, historical or deliberately out of scope.
Live / shippedpublic product, store release or functioning deployed service
Repositorycurrent implementation and named tests exist; does not imply tests were rerun today
Auditread-only forensic inspection tied to branch/commit or source snapshot
Historicaluseful evidence that must be labelled with its date and scope
WIP / recheckdesign or implementation exists but stronger public wording waits for verification
